How to take the SHSAT

What actually changes a score, beyond doing your best
← Practice
The three that matter most. Answer every question, because a blank scores exactly the same as a wrong answer. Never spend more than two minutes on one question. And take the whole test start to finish, timed, several times before the real day, because the test is as much endurance as knowledge.

1. The rules you can use

  • No penalty for guessing. Your score is the count of right answers. The DOE's own guide says omitting a question "will not give you an advantage." A one-in-four guess is worth a quarter point; a blank is worth zero.
  • You choose the order. You may start with ELA or Math and split the 180 minutes however you like. Decide before test day. Most people should start with their stronger section while fresh, bank it, and know how much time is left for the other.
  • No calculator, scrap paper provided. Every math problem is built to be done by hand. If your arithmetic is getting ugly, you have probably taken a wrong turn.
  • You cannot go back the way you used to. On the 2026 computer-adaptive test, Math is forward-only, and in ELA you can move around within a passage set but not after you leave it. The old trick of skipping hard ones and circling back is gone. Decide, answer, move on.
  • The difficulty adapts. Right answers bring harder questions, wrong ones easier. That is the test working as designed, not a sign something is wrong. Harder questions appearing means you are doing well.

2. Pacing

One hundred questions in 180 minutes is about 108 seconds each, but the sections are not equal.

  • The two-minute rule. At two minutes with no answer in sight, eliminate what you can, pick, and go. Four minutes on one hard question costs two or three easier ones later, and those are worth exactly the same.
  • Wear a watch (not a smart watch) or use the on-screen clock. Check it at the end of every passage set and every ten math questions, not every question.
  • Slow down for the first few questions of each section. On an adaptive test, early accuracy sets the level you are routed to. A careless miss at the start costs more than one later.
  • Leave nothing blank at the end. With two minutes left, fill in every remaining question. The clock does not pause for that.

3. Reading passages

  • Read the whole passage once, carefully, before the questions. Not skimming: the DOE guide is explicit about this. Skimming saves a minute and then costs it back twice on every question that needs the main idea.
  • After each paragraph, say in a few words what it did. "Introduces the problem." "Gives the first experiment." "Disagrees with paragraph 3." Most questions are really asking which paragraph did what.
  • Write the gist in one sentence before the first question. If you cannot, you have not finished reading.
  • Answer from the passage only. Choices that are true in the world but not in the text are the most common trap. So are choices that are supported, but by a different paragraph than the question points to.
  • Predict before you look. Decide what the answer should say, then find the choice that matches. Reading four choices cold lets the wrong ones argue with you.
  • Use the tools. Highlighter, answer eliminator, notepad. Crossing out two choices on screen turns a guess from one-in-four into one-in-two.

4. Revising/Editing

  • Half of this part is about paragraphs, not commas. Topic sentence, concluding sentence, the sentence that does not belong, the transition. For those, say the paragraph's point in a phrase first; the right answer serves that point.
  • For the mechanics questions, read the sentence aloud in your head. A comma splice or a tense shift usually sounds wrong before you can name it.
  • "Which pair of revisions" questions: test each half. Three of the four choices break something that was fine. A choice is right only if both changes fix real errors.
  • Read the question before the boxed sentence in Part A, so you know whether you are hunting for a comma, a verb, or a vague pronoun.

5. Math

  • Solve it, then look at the choices. The DOE guide recommends this and it is right: choices are written to look like the answer you get from a common mistake. If your answer is not there, re-read the question before redoing the arithmetic. Then check whether your answer is just written differently, for example 5(3 + x)/9 versus 5/3 + 5x/9.
  • Backsolving. When the question asks for a number and the choices are numbers, start from the middle choice and test it. Two tries usually settle it.
  • Pick a number. When the choices contain variables, pick an easy value such as 2, compute the question's answer, and see which choice matches. Avoid 0 and 1, which make too many choices agree.
  • Read the last line first. It tells you what is being asked: units, "how many more," "the larger integer," "in square feet." Then read the setup. Answering the wrong question is the single commonest miss.
  • Write every step down. No calculator means the arithmetic is the test. Mental shortcuts are where the sign errors live.
  • Fill-ins are not harder, just unforgiving. With no choices to sanity-check against, re-read the question once before entering the number, and make sure it is in the form asked for (a decimal, a fraction, degrees).
  • Don't cancel too early. A frequent trap is an answer that is correct but unfinished, one step short of the simplest form.

6. Where points are worth the most

The raw-to-scaled curve is steeper at the top. Going from 44 to 51 right on a section is worth about 30 scaled points; going from 51 to 57 is worth about 40. In your strong section, the last few questions are the most valuable on the whole test. Finishing a strong section carefully beats rescuing a weak one.

Both sections count equally in the composite, so there is no reason to drop one. But if you have to choose where the extra ten minutes go, they go to the section you are already good at.

7. Before the day

  • Take at least two full tests in this app under test conditions: full screen, the clock running, no stopping. The first one teaches you about the third hour.
  • Try the DOE's Student Readiness Tool so the screen, the highlighter, the eliminator, and the fill-in box are familiar. Nothing on test day should be new.
  • Sleep. A tired eighth grader loses more points to careless reading than to anything they never learned.
  • After every practice test, read the explanation on each miss and say in one sentence what you would do differently. That sentence is the part that sticks.
